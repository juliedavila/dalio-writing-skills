# Audience, register and persuasion

Writing resonates when the reader feels it was written for them: it starts from what they care about, uses their words, gives them the amount of context they need and no more, and asks for something they can actually do. This file covers how to work out who the reader is, how to pick the register, how to make a case, and what changes from channel to channel.

Much of this is practitioner craft rather than tested evidence, and the file says which is which. Where a rule is marked as inference, it is a reasonable extension of the research, not a finding.

## Working out the reader

Writers are poor at predicting what readers want. In field experiments, most people predicted a long email would outperform a short one and were wrong. Professional writers didn't share readers' preference for simpler headlines. So don't judge a draft by taste. Name the reader and judge it against them.

For each piece, answer four questions about the primary reader:

1. **What do they already know?** About the subject, the project, its history and its internal vocabulary. Assume they know nothing coined during the work.
2. **What do they care about?** Their goals, their metrics, what they'd lose. A CFO cares about cost, risk and exposure; a peer VP cares about their roadmap and their team's load; an engineer cares about what changes in their day.
3. **How aware are they of the problem?** Copywriter Eugene Schwartz's levels of awareness, adapted for internal writing (the adaptation is inference): a reader who doesn't know the problem exists needs the stakes first, with evidence. A reader who knows the problem but not your solution needs the solution and why it beats the alternatives. A reader who already supports it needs only the decision, the date and what you need from them. Giving a supporter the full case wastes their time; giving a newcomer only the ask loses them.
4. **What do you need them to do?** One action, decision or understanding, as specifically as possible.

Use the reader's own words for the problem when you can: phrasing from their goals, their team's retro, their Slack messages. Copywriters call this voice-of-customer research. Internally it means the reader recognizes their problem in the first line.

Secondary readers matter too. Anything sent to one executive may be forwarded to another, so it should make sense without the conversation it came from.

## Choosing the register

Register and length are separate settings. Every draft should be plain and as short as the reader's needs allow. The register, formal or conversational, is chosen by purpose:

- **Formal** for compliance, audit, regulators, external parties, policy, incident notices, risk acceptance and budget requests. In a large field study, formal letters drove more action than informal ones, apparently because formality signals that something is serious and credible, and comprehension didn't change. Applying this to internal bank memos is an inference, but a well-grounded one. Formal means complete sentences, no slang, no emoji, no jokes and few or no contractions. It does not mean long words, passive constructions or nominalizations.
- **Conversational** for peers, the author's own team, thanks and recognition, community posts and relationship-building. Contractions, "I" and "you", and some warmth. In a tone study of consumer copy, casual, conversational, moderately enthusiastic writing was rated friendlier and somewhat more trustworthy than formal copy.

In both registers, trust matters more than friendliness. In the same tone study, how trustworthy copy seemed explained about half the variation in whether people would recommend the organization, and friendliness added little. Humor lowered trust for a high-stakes product. So keep humor for wins and low-stakes notes. Emoji raise perceived warmth and lower perceived competence in work email, a result that has been replicated, so leave them out wherever competence matters.

On writing up, across and down: there's no verified research on how this should differ, so this is inference. Upward (managers, executives, boards), lead with the decision or the risk in their terms, quantify, state what you need and by when, and keep the detail available but out of the way. Across (peer leaders), lead with what's in it for their team, be explicit about what you're asking them to give up, and invite disagreement. Down (your own team), explain the why as well as the what, be specific about what changes for them, and be warm without being chummy about bad news.

## Making a case

For any proposal, request or recommendation, the classic structure from *Writing That Works* still holds: state the problem, state the solution, give the benefits in numbers, and answer the objections and the alternatives you rejected, including doing nothing. A proposal that lists only benefits reads as a sales pitch to a skeptical reader. State the cost next to the gain.

Three ways to frame it, depending on the reader:

- **Answer first** (Minto, BLUF): the recommendation, then the reasons, then the evidence. Best for decisions and busy readers. The risk is that a reader who rejects the answer may stop reading, so if you expect resistance, make sure the first reason is the strongest.
- **Situation, complication, question, answer (SCQA)**: a short story frame. The shared starting point, what changed, the question that raises, your answer. Good for a reader who doesn't yet see the problem.
- **Contrast between what is and what could be** (Nancy Duarte): move back and forth between the current state and the better one, naming what it costs to get there. A report that stays in the present bores; a pitch that stays in the future sounds like hype.

Lead with the data claim and add one representative example. Overall, stories and statistics persuade about equally, but the combination beats either alone, and statistics win when the argument needs to generalize. Avoid a vivid anecdote that isn't typical, because a skeptical reader will notice.

Persuasion principles used honestly (practitioner advice, adapted from Cialdini): show that peers or respected teams already do it, cite the expertise behind the recommendation, offer help before asking for it, and get commitments in writing. Never manufacture urgency or scarcity with colleagues ("only a few slots left", "before it's too late"), and don't use curiosity-gap teasers that hold back the point. Peers recognize the technique and trust the sender less.

## Channel notes

**Email.** The subject line states the point or the action ("Approval needed by Fri: pre-merge scanning for payments"). Industry data suggests short subject lines get more replies, but it's correlational, and one field experiment found a clearer subject line got fewer opens, so the reason for a clear subject line is that it respects the reader, not that it will boost opens. The ask goes in the first two or three sentences. Aim for something that fits on one screen. One topic per email; if there are two unrelated asks, send two emails. Put whatever the reader needs to act (the link, the deadline, the options) right next to the ask.

**Slack and chat.** The first line must work alone, because it's what shows in the notification and the channel view. Put the ask or the news there. Details go in the thread or a linked doc. Don't post a wall of bullets into a channel. In threads, answer the question asked before adding context.

**Executive and board updates.** Lead with the decision, the change in risk, or the ask. Quantify in business terms. Words of probability get numbers. State what would change your recommendation. Keep technical detail out of the body and leave out anything exploitable. Formal register.

**Announcements.** Lead with what's changing for the reader and when, then why, then what they need to do. Skip "we're excited to announce". If the news is good, the news carries the enthusiasm.

**Bad news.** Say it in the first sentence, in plain words, with who owns the fix and when the reader will hear more. Corporate euphemism ("underinvested", "rightsizing", "learnings") exists to soften bad news and readers know it, so it costs trust. Take responsibility in the active voice: "we missed the deadline", not "the deadline was not met." Formal register for incidents and anything external.

**Thanks and recognition.** Be specific about what the person did and what it made possible. "Thanks for all your hard work" is interchangeable; "your rewrite of the on-call runbook got the 2 a.m. page resolved in ten minutes instead of an hour" is not. Conversational register.

**LinkedIn posts, talk abstracts, community writing.** Here a hook is legitimate: the first sentence's only job is to make someone read the second (copywriter Joseph Sugarman's advice). Open with a specific claim, a number or a concrete moment, not a generic truth. Keep the specificity and the author's voice; this is where generic AI smoothness costs most.

**Bios.** Lead with what the person does now and for whom, then the one or two things that make them credible, in plain words. Third person for conference programs, first person for personal sites.

## Headlines and subject lines

A quick practitioner test, adapted from copywriter Bob Bly's "4 U's": is the line useful to the reader, specific (ideally with a number or a name), unique to this message, and, where honest, time-bound? Most internal subject lines fail on specificity. "Update" fails all four; "Pre-merge scanning cut critical vulns 40% in payments: approval to expand by Fri" passes. This has no response-rate evidence behind it, so use it as a prompt, not a score.
